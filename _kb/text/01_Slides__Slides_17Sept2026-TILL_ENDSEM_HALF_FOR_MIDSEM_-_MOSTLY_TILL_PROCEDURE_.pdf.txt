Higher Level Languages as Abstractions
▶The “height” of a language is its closeness to human
“level”—that means, its expressive power.
▶The language should let us express our intention;
▶and provide a toolchain to turn that intention into
computation;
▶with the least burden on the programmer for the “how” part;
▶and with as much fidelity—actual computation effected
should realise the original intention—as possible.
This view calls natural-language-like abstraction as the highest
level and machine code as the lowest.

Abstraction Concretised
It is not our wont to characterise abstraction in abstract
terms. We can only give concrete examples of abstraction of
ideas from concrete experience.
Content is concrete, form is abstract. That’s why, though we find
scarcely a perfect circle or ball, we call everything from a cylinder to a
rectangle with angular corners “round”—even persons with shoulder
width comparable to their height are called “round”.
▶The name of a library function, or its signature; a class in a standard
library, etc. are abstractions of the computations they promise.
▶Real numbers are abstract, floating point computations are concrete.
▶Placement tests and interviews on OOP are abstract, an OO program
designed and developed by a real programmer is concrete.

The Mechanics of Computation
▶The von Neumann machine is mechanical computation. Mechanical
computation means something we can emulate exactly (albeit extremely
slowly) using coarse, macro level devices like flipping switches: clearly
discernible elements of the interaction of which is broken into steps that
we can carry out without using any discretion or horse sense.
▶For example, we can demonstrate any sorting algorithm on a bunch of
books as if the bunch is an array. We can use graph algorithms for the
treasure hunt game in the Campus. If we switch lamps on doors in a
maze selectively on and off, we can guide a live mouse trained with those
lights to follow a maze navigation algorithm exactly.
▶Industrial robots doing repetitive tasks by limbs with limited degree of
freedom are based on mechanical computation – controlled by
Programmable Logic Controllers programmed using Ladder Diagrams.
▶Quantum computation is not mechanical in this classical sense.
Semi-autonomous robots are examples of a combination of mechanical
computation and human discretion.

What’s the Debate About? I
Long before he got the Turing Award, Dijkstra decried the use of
gotos in structured programming in Dijkstra [1968] and Rice and
Dijkstra [1968]. In his Turing Award acceptance speech, Dijkstra
said, inter alia, that
▶I/O interrupts, occuring at unpredictable and irreproducible
moments, compared with the old sequential machine that
pretended to be a fully deterministic automaton, has been a
dramatic change, creating grave logical problems.
▶Secondly, multilevel stores require extensive documentation
that still cannot solve the elusive complications of managing
them.
▶Above all, the inncreased power of hardware, together with
the perhaps even more dramatic increase in its reliability,
made solutions feasible that the programmer had not dared to
dream about a few years before.

What’s the Debate About? II
▶Programs–even alternative programs for the same task and
with the same mathematical content–can differ tremendously
in their intellectual manageability.
▶A number of rules have been discovered, necessary for
sustaining intellectual manageability of programs.
▶These rules are of two kinds. Those of the first kind are easily
imposed by the choice of a programming paradigm and a
compiler. Examples are the exclusion of goto-statements and
of procedures with more than one output parameter.
▶The second kind are elements of discipline required from the
programmer. Algol-60 was [to be] designed so as to enforce
this discipline at the outset.

Okay, “Practitioners” Respond
DuWorks and Smoliar [1972]:
One thing is certain: science has arisen from man’s quest to formulate terse generalizations of his myriad
observations. What is important is that observation precedes generalization–preferably by as great a distance as
possible. As Suzanne (Langer) has written:
General theories should be constructed by generalization from the principles of a special field, known
and understood in full detail. Where no such systematic order exists to serve as a pattern, a general
theory is more likely to consist of vague generalities than of valid generalizations.
▶“Cart before the Horse” : Du Works and Smoliar thought (in 1972) that
Dijkstra’s “formal” foundations of theory constitute a clear-cut
cart-before the-horse case of observation-generalization inversion.
▶ALGOL-60 v/s PL/I : ALGOL-60’s designers’ insistence on mathematical purity
was “formal overkill” causing some good ideas to be “aborted” or “still born”.
That conflict between a desire for mathematical purity and a need for “good
progranwting habits” could only yield a rather anomalous specimen such as
ALGOL-60. On the other hand, PL/I, provided a far superior language,
complete with the “generative ideas” of third-generation computers.
▶What their generation lacked most of all, in their opinion, was the demand for
“system elegance”, which is independent of, without necessarily excluding,
mathematical “elegance”.
▶So their caveat to make sure that the modeled object does not become
dominated by the model itself.

Empirical, not Analytical
Thus, we see computer science
and engineering, programming
paradigms, programming
langauges, and the conceptual
integrity and discipline required
of programmers and computer
scientists and engineers as
empirical, like physics, and not
analytical, like mathematics.
It is not that apples fall because Newton defined gravity that way; rather,
Newton defined gravity that way such that it can consistently explain
apples falling.
Moreover, Newton’s Law of Gravitation does not preclude rockets
escaping the earth’s gravitational pull. In fact it predicts such a
possibility and offers exact calculations to make it happen.
We should look at programming language paradigms and syntax in an
analogous manner: they are enablers and provide a scaffolding for (not
fetters against) building software systems.

▶Historically programmers identified repeatedly useful pieces of
code and bundled them as “macros” with meaningful
code-names (“mnemonics”) to be shared and quickly placed

beyond mnemonics and formula translation (ForTran),
structured programming langauges arose and facilitated
top-down design.
* Procedural to OO :
▶Proliferation of adaptive structured programs using common
operations repetitively revealed the underlying unity of types
and operations, leading to Object-oriented programming

Modular Programming III
Lessons:
1. Each compilation unit is independent in terms of type names,
variable names and function names. These names (and
structures, for types) have to be supplied to each compilation
unit afresh.
2. It is possible to create inconsistencies in data structures using
this independence.
3. We can make use of this flexibility to create data structures
with due information hiding for system security.
4. But it can lead to logical bugs as well. To avoid that, header
(*.h) files are used as common includes in various source
(*.c) files.
Among the course materials are more programs that exemplify
these points and explain them rigorously.

The Theoretical Basis of Structured Programming
The B ohm and Jacopini [1966] provides the theoretical basis of
structured programming. It states that three ways of combining
programs—sequencing, selection, and iteration—are sufficient to
express any computable function. This observation did not
originate with the structured programming movement; these
structures are sufficient to describe the instruction cycle of a
central processing unit, as well as the operation of a Turing
machine. Therefore, a processor is always executing a ”structured
program” in this sense, even if the instructions it reads from
memory are not part of a structured program.
The CPU mentioned above is our von Neumann machine.

Procedural Modular v/s OO
Parnas [1972] presents two modularizations for a small text
processor (called an “example system” in it). One modularization
is of the “conventional type” in the sense meant by all proponents
of modular programming: The system is divided into a number of
modules with well-defined interfaces; each one is small enough and
simple enough to be thoroughly understood and well programmed.
The other modularization or decomposition was made using
“information hiding” as a criterion. The modules no longer
correspond to steps in the processing. Every module in the second
decomposition is characterized by its knowledge of a design
decision which it hides from all others. Its interface or definition
was chosen to reveal as little as possible about its inner workings.
We can call this (rudimentary) OO decomposition.

The MetaObject Protocol I
Modern programming language design lives in tension between two
apparently conflicting demands. On the one hand, high-level lan-
guages such as Scheme, Prolog, and ML incorporate significant ad-
vances in elegance and expressive power. On the other hand, many
industrial programmers find these languages too ”theoretical” or im-
practical for everyday use, and too inefficient. As a result, these lan-
guages are often used only in academic and research contexts, while
the majority of the world’s mainline programming is conducted in
such languages as C and C++, distinguished instead for their effi-
ciency and adaptability.
This book is about a new approach to programming language de-
sign, in which these two demands of elegance and efficiency are
viewed as compatible, not conflicting. Our goal is the development
of languages that are as clean as the purest theoretical designs, but
that make no compromises on performance or control over imple-
mentation.

The MetaObject Protocol II
The way in which we have achieved elegance and efficiency jointly
is to base language design on metaobject protocols.
Metaobject
protocols are interfaces to the language that give users the ability to
incrementally modify the language’s behavior and implementation,
as well as the ability to write programs within the language.
Languages that incorporate metaobject protocols blur the distinction
between language designer and language user. Traditionally, design-
ers are expected to produce languages with well-defined, fixed behav-
iors (or ”semantics”). Users are expected to treat these languages as
immutable black-box abstractions, and to derive any needed flexibil-
ity or power from constructs built on top of them. This sharp division
is thought to constitute an appropriate division of labor. Program-
ming language design is viewed as a difficult, highly-specialized art,
inappropriate for average users to engage in. It is also often assumed
that a language design must be rigid in order to support portable
implementations, efficient compilers, and the like.

CLOS Program Structure
▶In its simplest terms, a CLOS program consists of defclass,
defgeneric, and defmethod forms mixed in with other more
traditional Common Lisp forms. Executing these forms defines
the program’s classes, generic functions and methods.
▶Backstage, execution of these forms creates internal
representations of the classes, generic functions, and methods,
recording the information provided in their definitions. The
implementation uses the information stored in the internal
representation of a class to create instances of that class and
to access their slots. Information stored in the internal
representation of a generic function and its methods is used to
invoke the generic function.

Method Combinations
▶Under the rules of standard method combination, applicable
before-methods are executed first, from most specific to
least specific. The most specific applicable primary method is
executed next, followed by the applicable after-methods,
from least specific to most specific. The value(s) returned by
the call to the generic function are the values(s) returned by
the primary method.
▶The function apply-methods does this sequencing, calling
apply-method to invoke individual methods on the
arguments.
▶Applying a method is handled in basically the same way a Lisp
interpreter handles a call to a normal (non-primitive) function:
evaluate the body form in the lexical environment of the
defmethod as extended with new variable binding resulting
from matching the lambda list against the list of actual
arguments.

Engineer academicians working on an industrial project of design and development of a metaprogramming language
with Europolitan Vodafone AB and Diedata AB say:
Reflection
There are two types of reflection:
introspection and
intercession ...
The purpose of introspection is to
acquire information about the program itself and to
use that information within the program.
Intercession goes further.
It allows the program to
alter its own behavior.
Different programming
languages have different reflection capabilities.
Languages such as Lisp or Smalltalk have both
introspection and intercession, while Java is
basically introspective only.

Performance v/s Expressive Power
▶Naive implementations of the MOP are woefully inefficient.
At the very least, any real implementation must handle
generic function invocation and slot access more efficiently.
▶The basis for optimizing implementations is the idea: improve
performance by computing the results of critical internal
computations once and then saving and reusing that
result. This technique is known as memoization.
