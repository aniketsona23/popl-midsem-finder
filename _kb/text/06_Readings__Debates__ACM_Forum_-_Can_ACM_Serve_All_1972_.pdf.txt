Can ACM Serve All? 
In his ACM President's Letter in 
the February 1972 issue of Com- 
munications, 1 Walter Carlson has by 
ellipsis misquoted the U.S. Depart- 
ment of Labor, and so caused Com- 
puter Science academicians much un- 
necessary grief. 
The Department of Labor said ~ 
"At the present time the computer sci- 
ences are not generally recognized by 
colleges and universities as a bona fide 
academic discipline with standardized li- 
censing, certification, or registration pro- 
cedures." (Italics supplied.) 
By omitting the words italicized 
above, President Carlson changed 
the meaning quite significantly. With 
those words in place, the statement 
is far more appropriate to the pur- 
poses of the Department of Labor, 
and seems quite true, if a bit ungram- 
matical. 
Computer Science is not recog- 
nized as an academic profession, for 
there is no special disciplinary ac- 
creditation of Computer Science cur- 
ricula, as there is for engineering, 
pharmacy, law, and other profes- 
sional curricula. In most colleges and 
universities, Computer Science is a 
nonprofessional discipline in the 
School of Arts and Sciences, or in 
the Graduate School. Left to its own 
devices, academic Computer Science 
will undoubtedly opt to remain in 
the relative freedom of the Arts and 
Sciences, rather than be constrained 
to a professional mold, but that is 
another subject. 
The spokesman for the Depart- 
ment of Labor was slightly muddied, 
in any case, to confuse academic 
Computer Science with preparation 
specifically for jobs in programming 
or systems analysis, but that confu- 
sion is widely held. A Computer Sci- 
ence major today, whether at bache- 
lor or master level, is being prepared 
not just to program, or to analyze 
real world problems in preparation 
for programming, but rather: 
• 
to improve the tools that pro- 
grammers and systems analysts use, 
• 
to develop better machine sys- 
tems, better software systems, better 
languages for communicating with 
the machines, and better methods for 
solving intractable problems, 
• 
to manage activities that are 
strongly computer dependent, 
• 
to teach about computers at col- 
lege level, or 
• 
to advance the fundamental 
theory of digital information proces- 
SOl-S. 
Some graduates of Computer Science 
programs may be programmers or 
systems analysts, but they will have 
changed direction, or missed their 
aim, or simply still be in their learn- 
ing phase. 
How then does this affect ACM? 
The essential question is whether 
ACM is the Society of the computer 
scientists, or of the programmers and 
systems analysts, or of both. To be 
both is a greater challenge, and has 
significant financial and operational 
advantages, but requires a continu- 
ing recognition on the part of ACM 
that there are marked differences be- 
tween the two camps, and that each 
must be served well if the Associa- 
tion is to prosper in the future as it 
has in the past. 
Howard E. Tompkins

Discuss Impact of Computer on 
Society at Annual Meetings? 
On Kent Curtis' point, 1 which 
seems to be that discussion of the im- 
plications and consequences of com- 
puting at ACM annual meetings is not 
appropriate to a professional society, 
I would say that this view is very 
widely, and very unfortunately, held 
throughout the computing commun- 
ity and within the ACM itself. It seems 
to me that if computing is a profes- 
sion, and if we are professionals, then 
we must come to grips, through dis- 
cussions at annual meetings and in 
official publications and elsewhere, 
with problems of the impact of the 
computer on society. It is just this 
concern which distinguishes the pro- 
fessional from the technician, and 
the profession from the trade. In the 
words of Anthony Wedgwood Benn, 
sometime [British] Minister of Tech- 
nology, 
It is high time cyberneticists and Ph.Ds. 
in math, and programmers and systems 
analysts, and hardware and software ex- 
perts, come out of their secret world. 
They must.., begin assuming citizenship 
responsibilities for the consequences of 
what they are doing. They must also 
understand the implications of all they 
are planning for us. For it could be the 
most revolutionary onslaught ever made 
by any group of men on any established 
society. 2 
If we should have these con- 
cerns, it seems to me that our profes- 
sional society should be a focal point 
for their expression, just as it is in 
the area of technical concerns.
