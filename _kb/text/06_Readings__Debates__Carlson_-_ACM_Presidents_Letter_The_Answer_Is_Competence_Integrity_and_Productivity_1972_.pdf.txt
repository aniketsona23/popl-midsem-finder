ACM 
President's Letter 
The Answer Is... 
Competence, Integrity, and Productivity 
...What Is the Question? 
The question is "What defines a 
prolessional systems analyst or pro- 
grammer?" 
We now have legally important 
answers to this question from the 
Wage and Hour Division of the U.S. 
Department of Labor and from the 
National Labor Relations Board.* 
These two regulatory bodies have 
found that persons with job titles of 
"programmer" or "systems analyst" 
are not classifiable as proJessionals 
under the definitions used in regula- 
tions that determine status for over- 
time pay and collective bargaining. 
The impact on ACM may be im- 
portant in the long term. 
Let's look at a few facts that in- 
dicate why: • Nearly 55 percent of 
ACM members are directly involved 
in programmingor systems analysis. A 
crucial factor in the regulatory rulings 
is the lack of availability of a bac- 
calaureate degree in programming or 
systems analysis. While 92 percent 
of ACM members have college degrees, 
not 1 percent have such a degree in 
programming or systems analysis. • 
The Wage and Hour Division also 
found that "the computer sciences 
are not generally recognized by col- 
leges and universities as a bona fide 
academic discipline .... " * Another 
crucial factor is the lack of availability 
of a formal certification process in the 
fields covered. Of ACM members 87 
percent hold no professional certifi- 
cate of any kind; 6.5 percent have 
the CDP issued by DPMA, 6 percent 
have professional engineering regis- 
trations, and the remainder hold CPAS 
or miscellaneous other licenses. 
These indicators appear to place 
• See page 129 and 125 of this issue. 
ACM squarely at the crossroads which 
has been occupied, uncomfortably, 
by a number of other technical so- 
cieties during the past year or two: 
Do we remain strictly a technical so- 
ciety in accordance with the present 
charter? or Do we become a society 
having a primary concern for the 
welfare of its members? There have 
been strong voices raised within ACM 
urging a move toward this latter mode 
of operation similar to a Guild. 
The actions by the Department 
of Labor, the NLRB, and the courts 
have convinced me beyond doubt 
that the future of ACM is dependent 
upon retaining the role of a technical 
society. Doing so may, in fact, pro- 
vide the only viable answers to the 
question of what defines status in 
programming and systems analysis. 
By this, I mean that the day may be 
close when employers and others may 
make the determination of legal status 
solely on the basis of whether the 
programmer or systems analyst in- 
volved is a member of ACM--and 
therefore technically qualified. Along 
the way we must also work to correct 
the public impression that computer 
science is not a learned profession. 
The ACM membership adopted a 
constitutional change last year that 
clearly paves the way to a determi- 
nation of legal status. By declaring 
competence and integrity of our mem- 
bers to be essential to continued 
achievement of ACM'S objectives, we 
have set in motion the steps necessary 
to achieve two components of the 
answer in the title above. 
But assurances of competence and 
integrity are not enough in the par- 
ticular instance of programmers and 
systems analysts. A third component, 
productivity, is at least equally im- 
portant. 
The decline of programmer prod- 
uctivity over the past decade, if con- 
tinued, could approach the propor- 
tions of a national scandal. A num- 
ber of key people (Herb Grosch's and 
Dick Hamming's names leap to mind) 
have issued public warnings, but little 
has been done about their concerns. 
Beneath these concerns lies an 
urgent issue. Who among today's pro- 
grammers and systems analysts is 
engaged in a disciplined, personal 
effort to improve the tools, tech- 
niques, organizations, and attitudes 
that determine their own productivity 
and that of their associates, i.e. the 
true professional? Conversely, who 
among these people is conducting or- 
ganized efforts which effectively re- 
strict the quantity and quality of 
programming activities, i.e. the non- 
professional? 
My discussions with ACM mem- 
bers lead me to believe that we have 
as members a predominance of the 
people who are trying to improve 
productivity. 
I would like to see ACM begin a 
program that achieves the qualifica- 
tion of every ACM member for suit- 
able status in the eyes of the law and 
in the eyes of society in general. Such 
status is not granted-it is earned. 
The project will be a difficult one. 
Our associates in the British Compu- 
ter Society have found a way to 
achieve this goal. The Canadians 
have begun action. 
The question is asked in the U.S. 
Let ACM give the answer! 
-W.M. Carlson
