Short Communications 
Education 
Computer Science-A Vicious 
Circle 
Abraham Kandel 
New Mexico Institute of Mining and Technology* 
Key Words and Phrases: education, computer 
engineering, computer science curriculum, systems 
design 
CR Categories: 1.52, 6.0 
In many computer science departments throughout 
the country, including some interdisciplinary depart- 
ments, the curriculum has placed increasing emphasis 
upon applied mathematics and the fundamental nature 
of computational systems. A set of core courses which 
usually are fitted into such a program can be found in 
the report of the ACM curriculum committee on com- 
puter science, Curriculum 68 [1]. Some computer 
science departments have done such a magnificent job 
of de-emphasizing the importance of the experimental 
laboratory in their program that their graduates emerge 
thoroughly unprepared to tackle the intricacies asso- 
ciated with design work in the real-life world--both in 
software and hardware. This is basically because most 
computer science departments do not emphasize suffi- 
ciently the practical aspects of computational system 
design and implementation. For example, some stu- 
dents have serious difficulty in designing even a shift 
register of an 8-4-2-1 counter with ripple carry, not to 
mention more complicated computer systems. Perhaps 
even more important, computer science in the aca- 
demic sense has implemented the "modus ponens" 
(rule of detachment) from the real-life world and has 
very little to do with real computers. The relationship 
between the abstract side of computer science and 
questions of cost and marketing are hardly ever men- 
tioned in courses. 
The increasing gap between the abstract and applied 
aspects of computer science turns the problem of

No doubt there is much beauty in an abstract 
approach to computer science. Eventually, we hope, 
this approach will benefit the computer industry and, 
ultimately, our society. However, we must take the 
right steps in training computer science students to 
benefit the industry now. This can easily be accom- 
plished by combining theoretical courses with a prac- 
tical approach to computer engineering. 
Industry gets graduates from computer science 
departments with a bag full of the latest technical 
jargon but no depth of understanding of real computer 
systems and with no concept of the problems they will 
be asked to face and solve. Computer science graduates 
have programmed only a limited set of problems in 
computer languages, many of which they have never 
finished or tested adequately. The average graduate 
has no solid idea of what happens when design projects 
get large, as most real-life problems do. In this category 
we include large systems design (hardware and soft- 
ware), file management and efficient implementation of 
complex programs which exceed available core storage 
or take a long time to execute. Most of these graduates 
are not well prepared to fill the needs of the computer 
industries, business, and governmental agencies, be- 
cause their background as computer science majors is 
generally inadequate for this purpose. 
It is quite obvious that there is no effective theory 
of computer science as such. In fact, there are no effec- 
tive models of computers. However some theories 
seem to fit naturally into the scope of the theoretical 
aspects of computer science. For example, there are 
the theories of automata, formal languages, computa- 
tional complexity, combinatorics, systems, applied ab- 
stract algebra (Boolean algebra, lattices, semigroups, 
universal algebra), metamathematics, coding, and algo- 
rithm theory. Usually courses are approached through 
theory and models. However, in several cases (courses 
in computer system organization or computer design), 
a case-study approach on a large-scale project would 
help the student to understand the formalism of the 
subject and to see precisely its entire practical content. 
Case studies provide examples of good standard prac- 
tice and documentation and reflect modern design 
features. (By "design features" we mean hardware and 
software design, as it is our purpose not to separate 
these two aspects.) 
Most courses attempt to satisfy the following gen- 
eral goals: (a) develop a solid design methodology; (b) 
present the relevant key ideas and their implementation;

and (c) develop familiarity with the basic techniques of

However, most of them do not attempt to gain 
experience in grappling with life-like, large complex 
systems. It is true that computer science students may 
perform experimental work in hardware design, with 
algorithms, programs, and applications. But it should 
be clear that this is not enough; the student should also 
achieve real understanding of the inherent problems 
and their implications. Only then can he try to utilize 
his knowledge by looking at the details of the tech- 
niques used to implement his system. The system should 
be a real working one, in which good standard practice 
and state-of-the-art techniques should be used. The 
first step in achieving this is to change the dialogue 
between the commercial user of computer science per- 
sonnel and the computer science educator from an imi- 
tation into a practical path. It is important that the 
vicious circle between computer science in the aca- 
demic environment and the computer industry should 
be closed. 
Even though much of the complexity of modern 
computer systems is due to the need for efficiency in 
the design, we should teach the prospective designer 
not to worry about how well the computational system 
works before determining whether it will work at all. 
Then, consideration of efficiency and other real-life 
constraints (cost, marketing, etc.) must be emphasized 
until a real-life design is achieved by the gradual re- 
moval of the unrealistic assumptions which build the 
first layer in the student's studies. In order to reinforce 
this development and to lend credibility to the system 
design (hardware or software), the student's work 
must be complemented by a practical, carefully-designed 
case study. One attempt to go quite far in this direction 
has been made in the design of the UHC- 1 at the Univer- 
sity of Hawaii [2]. As pointed out in the report on the 
UHC-I, it has been possible for a group of students to 
design something meaningful with emphasis on doing 
rather than on learning how to do. 
Therefore, it is recommended that design projects, 
such as the minicomputer UHC-1, from basic specifica- 
tions to complete circuitry and machine language, be 
performed in a senior-level course on computer design 
in order to stimulate student interest in computer-based 
industries, to increase communication between groups 
of students on a design team (after all, industry is 
based on a team effort), and to expose the students to 
real-life constraints (cost is "sometimes" a prime con- 
sideration). 
Conclusion 
I. Since computer science is an applied science, it 
seems wise that computer science departments main- 
tain a close contact with computer-based industries. 
2. The basic difference between stimulating student 
interest toward graduate work or toward work in the 
computer industry should be taken into account when 
a computer science curriculum is designed by a depart- 
ment. 
3. The industry requires not only people who can 
fill jobs that have sophisticated requirements but also 
people who have the know-how to implement their 
background in the most practical way. 
4. The amalgamation of theory and the practical 
approach provides the computer science department 
with a means of access to a wide variety of languages, 
techniques, and systems without major capital and 
operating expenditures.

Editorial Comments by Peter Wegner 
Professor Kandel expresses the widely felt need 
among computer professionals for a greater practical 
component to computer science education. However, 
the inadequacy of practical training should not be 
blamed completely on theoreticians. The development 
of sound practical training programs appears to be 
much harder than the development of theoretical 
courses or even of core courses such as those in Cur- 
riculum 68 [1]. Practical training programs require a 
great deal of instructor's time and computer time. 
Moreover we do not know how to teach "good" pro- 
gramming habits. It is clear that we are not currently 
providing an optimal computer science education, 
either for practical programmers or for theorists. The 
reasons for this may, however, be more subtle than an 
overemphasis of theory. A number of computer science 
departments have devoted considerable effort to prac- 
tical training programs, both internally and in coopera- 
tion with industry, but no program yet developed has 
been implemented by a large number of computer 
science departments. We may have been teaching the 
wrong kind of theory rather than too much theory. 
The right kind of theory is likely to have considerable 
relevance, just as in the case of engineering. Our prob- 
lem is that we have not yet discovered the right kind 
of theoretical or practical basis for training computer 
scientists. When we do, it may well turn out that the 
theoretical and practical components of a computer 
science education will be more closely interrelated than 
they are at present. Further discussion of the questions 
raised by Professor Kandel is invited.
